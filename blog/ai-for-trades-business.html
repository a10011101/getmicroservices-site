AI for Tradespeople: Stop Losing Evenings to Admin

How plumbers, electricians, and landscapers are using AI to schedule jobs, send quotes, follow up on leads, and get their evenings back.

If you run a plumbing, electrical, or landscaping business, the day ends twice. Once when you pack the van. Again at the kitchen table, when the missed calls, the unsent quotes, and tomorrow's run are still waiting. This is a plain-English guide to the six admin jobs trades owners are handing off right now, in the order they usually pay back the fastest. You do not need to learn new software. You need to name the job that is eating the evening.

Missed calls while you are on the tools

On a busy day a plumber or electrician misses 6 to 10 calls. You are under a sink, in a ceiling void, or halfway through a mower service with the radio up. The caller rarely leaves a useful voicemail. About 3 in 4 of those people book someone else if nobody replies within 5 minutes. They wanted a person, or at least a clear sign that a person will ring back before tea.

The fix is a text that goes out within 60 seconds of the missed call. Keep it short and in your voice. Sorry we missed you. Reply with the job and a time window, and we will confirm tonight. If you already let people pick a slot themselves, that link can sit in the same message. About half of those missed leads reply. For a one-van operator, 2 extra jobs a week at a typical £220 for the call-out plus labour is about £440 a week, roughly £1,700 a month. The evening voicemail ritual drops from 30 to 45 minutes to a 5-minute scan of who already wrote back.

You still choose who you ring. The text does not book a job you cannot do, and it does not promise a same-day emergency if the board is full. It stops the lead going cold while your hands are full. The same pattern covers the texts and emails that pile up between jobs. We walk through that in AI customer support automation.

Landscapers feel this one differently. A homeowner calls at lunch about a patio, gets no answer, and books a firm that picked up. By the time you listen to the voicemail at 7pm, the lead has had two other site visits booked. A 60-second text keeps you in the running without pulling you off the mower.

Quotes that die in the van

You priced the job on site. The numbers are in a notebook, a photo of a scribbled card, or a voice note you made in the driveway. Writing that up takes 20 to 40 minutes once you are home, and it rarely happens the same day. Across the trades we see, 40 to 60 percent of quotes never get a follow-up at all. Most of those go cold after 48 hours. The customer has already had two other prices and picked the person who wrote back.

The useful version is boring, which is why it works. The quote goes out the same evening as a clear note: what you will do, what it costs, and when you can start. A reminder goes on day 2. Another goes on day 5. If it is seasonal, a last note asks whether they still want it done before the cold weather, the holiday rush, or the next stretch of rain. You approve the wording once. After that, the chase happens without you sitting down to remember who you visited on Tuesday.

Close rate on quotes that actually get followed up typically moves from about 25 percent to about 40 percent. A landscaper sending 8 quotes a week who wins 1 extra job at £600 to £1,200 has added that amount every week. Quote admin itself drops from 5 to 8 hours a week to about 90 minutes of checking drafts. Photos from the visit can sit with the price, so the customer remembers which fence, which consumer unit, or which leaking trap you were talking about. Building tomorrow's run at the kitchen table

Double bookings and "I will swing by after the Johnson job" cause 1 to 2 wasted trips a week for a lot of owner-operators. A wasted trip is 45 to 90 minutes plus fuel, often £40 to £80 in labour before you have even started the work the customer is paying for. The customer who waited is annoyed. The job you were meant to be on is now late. You find out from a text while you are already on the ring road.

A shared job list fixes the boring part. A new job lands, the travel between postcodes is checked, and the van that finishes nearest gets the slot. The customer gets a morning text with a 2-hour window, not a vague "sometime tomorrow". Emergency call-outs slot in by pushing the lowest-priority afternoon job and texting that customer a new window before you leave the current one. You stop building tomorrow's run at the kitchen table. That is 45 to 70 minutes back every evening. It is the difference between eating with your family and eating over a notebook.

Plenty of trades already keep the list in a spreadsheet, or in a job system such as ServiceM8 or Tradify, because that is what the office can open. You do not retype it. That list can drive the morning texts. We collected Google Sheets automation examples that match this kind of daily run.

Invoices and the Sunday night chase

A lot of one-van businesses invoice in a batch on Sunday, or they forget until the customer asks for the bill. A one-van electrician billing £8,000 to £12,000 a month feels that lag in the overdraft. When the invoice goes out the day the job is marked done, with a polite reminder at day 7 and another at day 14, average time to payment typically drops from about 28 days to about 12 days. That is about 16 days sooner. It is not extra turnover. It is your own money back in the account before the supplier bill lands.

The reminder copy stays polite. You are not a debt collector, and the customer who just had a new consumer unit fitted should not get a nasty note. Anything that looks odd comes back to you instead of going out: a dispute, a part still on order, a price you changed on site. If you already live in Xero or QuickBooks, the invoice can be raised there when the job is marked done. Nobody has to retype the same lines on Sunday. The hour you used to spend texting "just checking you got the invoice" at 10pm goes away. The longer version of this is in how to automate invoice processing.

Google reviews after a long day

The next boiler, rewire, or garden call is often decided by the Google reviews at the top of the listing. Unanswered ones, especially the 1 to 3 star reviews, sit where new customers can see them. Happy customers rarely write unless someone asks the evening the job is finished, while the house is warm, the lights all work, or the lawn looks right.

The setup watches for new reviews and drafts a reply in your voice. A 5-star review gets a short thank-you, ready for you to send. A complaint gets a draft that owns the issue, skips the grovelling, and offers a callback or a time to put it right. You still press send. Nothing goes out on its own. Review admin drops from 20 to 30 minutes a night to a 10-minute batch about three times a week. The same evening the job is done, the happy customer gets a short nudge asking if they would mind leaving a review. That is what grows the review count, not a poster in the van window.

Van stock and the second trip

The second trip is the quiet profit leak. You finish a tap, a socket, or a planting bed and realise the olive, the blank, or the irrigation fitting is in the lock-up, not the van. One unpaid or half-paid return visit a week costs about £50 to £90 in time and fuel, and it makes a good customer wonder if you are organised. The parts themselves are predictable. Plumbers run out of traps and olives. Electricians run out of cable and consumer unit blanks. Landscapers run out of membrane, mulch, and a fitting they used three times last week.

A weekly reorder list, built from what you actually fitted, lands in your inbox or goes to the trade counter as a draft you approve with one tap. If you note parts on the job sheet or in a simple spreadsheet, that note is enough. You still eyeball anything that varies by eye, like plants or a one-off stone. Standard fittings should not depend on your memory at 7pm on a Thursday. The Saturday morning counter run becomes one trip, not three stops because you forgot the olives again.

How to pick the first one

When the customer says yes, the next mess is usually their details. Address, parking, access notes, photos of the boiler, gate codes, where the stopcock is. That handover looks a lot like client onboarding, just with muddy boots instead of a slide deck. Do not start there if the phone is the thing losing you money this week.

  • If you miss calls while you are on the tools, start with the 60-second text. It often pays for itself inside two weeks.
  • If you are writing quotes at 10pm, start with same-evening quotes and the day-2 and day-5 chase.
  • Describe the job in plain English. What starts it, what should happen in the middle, and what done looks like.
  • Most of these are live in 3 to 7 days. You do not touch the setup. You check that the messages sound like you.
