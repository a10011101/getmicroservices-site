AI for Tradespeople: Stop Losing Evenings to Admin

How plumbers, electricians, and landscapers are using AI to schedule jobs, send quotes, follow up on leads, and get their evenings back.

Tools go back in the van around five. The paperwork does not. If you plumb, wire, or landscape for a living, the second shift is the missed call, the quote still in a notebook, tomorrow's run, and the invoice you meant to send on Tuesday. Here are six jobs owners hand off first, with the hours and the money attached, written for someone who would rather be on the tools than in a settings screen.

the call you could not take

A busy day means 6 to 10 calls you never hear. Your hands are in a ceiling, under a bath, or on a mower, and the phone is in a pocket you cannot reach. People do not wait. About 3 in 4 callers book another firm if they hear nothing within 5 minutes. By the time you play the voicemail in the driveway, the job has gone.

What works is a plain text inside 60 seconds. It sounds like you, not like a call centre. Sorry we missed you. Tell us the job and a window that suits, and we will confirm tonight. If you already offer a way to pick a slot, that can ride along in the same message. Roughly half of those missed leads answer. Two extra jobs a week, at about £220 for call-out and labour, is £440 a week. That is roughly £1,700 a month for a one-van business. Checking voicemail shrinks from 30 to 45 minutes at night to about 5 minutes of reading replies.

You keep the decision. A full board stays full. The message only holds the lead until you can talk. Customer texts and emails between jobs follow the same idea, covered in AI customer support automation. For a landscaper the sting is the lunchtime patio enquiry. No answer, and by evening they have two other firms walking the garden. The text keeps your name in that conversation without stopping the machine.

quotes that never get a second look

The price was agreed in the kitchen or at the bottom of the garden. Then it lives on a scrap of card, a photo, or a voice note, and turning it into something the customer can accept takes 20 to 40 minutes you do not have. In the businesses we sit with, 40 to 60 percent of those quotes are never chased. After 48 hours most of them are dead, because someone else sent a tidy price the same night.

The chase itself is simple. Same evening, they get the scope, the price, and a start date. Day 2, a short nudge. Day 5, another. Seasonal work gets one last ask: still want this done before the frost, the Christmas shutdown, or the wet week you both know is coming. You sign off the tone once. You stop trying to remember Tuesday's visits from a glovebox notebook.

Quotes that get this follow-up tend to close at about 40 percent instead of about 25 percent. Send 8 quotes a week and pick up 1 extra landscaping job at £600 to £1,200, and that figure repeats. The writing and chasing falls from 5 to 8 hours a week to about 90 minutes of approving drafts. A photo of the fence, the consumer unit, or the trap you are replacing sits with the price so they know which job they are saying yes to. If you want the wider order of jobs worth handing off, start with what small businesses usually automate first.

who is going where tomorrow

Owner-operators lose 1 to 2 trips a week to double bookings and to the plan that only exists in their head. "After the Johnson job" is not a plan when Johnson overruns. Each empty trip burns 45 to 90 minutes and often £40 to £80 in labour and fuel before a single paid minute starts. The waiting customer is cross. The job you skipped is now behind. You learn about it from a text on the ring road.

Put the jobs on one list the vans can share. When a new job arrives, travel between postcodes is checked and the van finishing closest takes it. The customer gets a morning message with a 2-hour window instead of "sometime". A genuine emergency bumps the least urgent afternoon visit, and that customer hears the new window before you leave the job you are on. The kitchen-table planning session, 45 to 70 minutes a night, stops being the way tomorrow gets built.

Your list might already be a spreadsheet, or it might already sit in ServiceM8 or Tradify. Leave it there. The morning texts can come from that list. Google Sheets automation examples show the same daily-list pattern if a sheet is what your office actually uses.

getting paid without another evening on the phone

Sunday night invoicing is normal in a one-van firm, and so is waiting until the customer chases you. An electrician turning over £8,000 to £12,000 a month carries that delay on the overdraft. Send the bill the day the job is marked done, then a polite reminder on day 7 and another on day 14, and payment typically moves from about 28 days to about 12 days. Sixteen days sooner is not new sales. It is cash you already earned, back before the wholesaler wants paying.

The wording stays decent. A household that just paid for a consumer unit should not get a threatening note. Odd cases stay with you: a row about the price, a part still to arrive, a discount you agreed in the hallway. If the books are in Xero or QuickBooks, the bill can be raised there as soon as the job is done, so Sunday is not a retyping session. The 10pm "did you see the invoice" texts disappear. More of the mechanics are in how invoice processing gets automated.

reviews that sit unanswered

Google decides a lot of the next boiler swap, rewire, or garden tidy. The reviews people see first are often the ones nobody answered, and a 1 to 3 star review with no reply does more damage than the star rating alone. Customers who were happy do not write unless you ask the evening the work finished, while the heating is on, the lights stay on, or the beds look finished.

New reviews get a draft in your own wording. Five stars become a short thanks you can send. A complaint becomes a draft that admits what went wrong, does not grovel, and offers a call back or a return visit. You press send. Nothing leaves on its own. The nightly 20 to 30 minutes of review admin becomes a 10-minute batch about three times a week. The request for a review goes out the evening the job closes, which is what actually grows the count. A sticker on the van does not.

the parts you forgot to reorder

Return visits are where margin leaks. The tap is in, the socket is on, the bed is planted, and the olive, the blanking plate, or the irrigation fitting is back at the lock-up. One half-paid or unpaid second trip a week is about £50 to £90 plus a customer who now doubts you. The missing items repeat. Traps and olives for plumbers. Cable and consumer unit blanks for electricians. Membrane, mulch, and last week's fitting for landscapers.

Once a week, a reorder list built from the parts you actually used shows up for a one-tap yes, or as a draft the trade counter can pick. Job notes or a basic sheet are enough input. You still look at anything you buy by eye, such as plants or a special stone. You should not be relying on memory at 7pm for fittings you use every day. Saturday at the counter becomes a single stop instead of a second and third run for the bits you forgot.

which job to hand off first

After a yes, the admin often shifts to collecting the address, parking notes, boiler photos, gate codes, and where the stopcock hides. That pack is the trades version of client onboarding. Useful, but the wrong first job if missed calls are the leak.

  • missed calls on the tools: start with the 60-second text. many firms cover the cost inside two weeks.
  • quotes still open at 10pm: send them the same evening, then chase on day 2 and day 5.
  • say it in normal language. what kicks it off, what should happen next, and what finished means.
  • most of these are running in 3 to 7 days. your job is to read the messages and confirm they sound like you.
