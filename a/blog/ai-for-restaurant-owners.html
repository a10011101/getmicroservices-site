AI for Restaurant Owners: Save 10 Hours a Week Without Hiring

How restaurant owners are using AI to handle bookings, cut food waste, speed up ordering, and keep staff focused on customers.

If you run a restaurant the week does not look like a tidy list of jobs. It looks like friday at 6pm with two no-shows, a one-star review you have not answered, a supplier text asking if you want the usual, and a menu change that still has not reached the delivery apps. This is a plain-english guide to the six jobs most restaurant owners are automating right now, in the order they usually pay back the fastest.

reservations and no-shows

No-shows are the easiest win because the fix pays for itself inside a month. The booking lands in your diary. A confirmation goes out by text one day before the sitting. A second message goes out two hours before with a one-tap confirm or cancel link. If they cancel the table is released and the standby list is pinged. If they do not reply you can decide whether to hold the table or chase the deposit.

Most places running this see no-shows drop from around 12 to 15 percent down to 3 to 5 percent. The money that used to walk out stays in the till. If you are not sure where the hours actually go, see which jobs small businesses typically automate first.

replying to google and tripadvisor reviews

Every unanswered review is a tiny public dent in your reputation. The automation watches your profiles for new reviews, scores sentiment, and drafts a reply in your voice. Five-star reviews get a short warm thank-you ready to send. One to three-star reviews come to your inbox with a draft that owns the problem, apologises without grovelling, and offers a specific next step like a direct email address.

You still press send. Nothing goes out automatically. The hour-a-day admin becomes a ten-minute coffee job. The same engine runs on your inbox for customer questions. Pattern covered in ai customer support automation.

keeping menus and prices in sync across delivery apps

Most owners we meet have four menus. The one in the kitchen, the one on the website, and two slightly different versions on the delivery platforms. When a dish 86s or a price goes up, three of those menus get updated and the fourth does not until a customer complains. The automation makes one of those menus the source of truth, usually your POS, and pushes every change to the website and the delivery platforms within minutes.

The best part is not the time saved. It is the chargebacks you stop losing to customer price disputes.

supplier reorders from stock levels

If you count stock on sunday night with a clipboard, this one is for you. Your POS already knows how much chicken you sold last week. A weekly job compares that against your pars, generates the order sheet, and either emails the supplier directly or drops a draft in your inbox for a one-tap send. For produce and anything you need to eyeball the clipboard still wins. For dry goods, drinks, and anything with a steady run rate the computer wins every week. If spreadsheets are a big part of your week start with these google sheets automation examples.

supplier invoices and reconciliations

Invoices arrive as PDFs, emails, and the odd paper copy stuffed into a delivery. Someone keys them into Xero or QuickBooks later. The automation reads the invoice, pulls out supplier, date, totals, and VAT, matches it against the purchase order, and posts it straight into your accounts. Anything that does not match comes to you with a note. Most restaurants we work with get back half a day a week on this alone. Deeper mechanics in automate invoice processing.

shift planning from cover counts

Your POS knows every cover you have served for the last two years. Last monday was raining and quiet. Last saturday was packed. Shift planning is still often done by gut. The automation looks at the last twelve weeks of covers for the same day of the week, adjusts for school holidays and weather, and suggests a shift pattern you can approve or edit. New managers get the benefit of your historical pattern without having to remember it. Owners get sunday nights back.

how to pick the first one

  • write down the one job that costs you money every week. for most places it is no-shows or stock
  • describe it in plain english: what triggers it, what happens in the middle, what the result should be
  • most single automations ship in 3 to 7 days. you do not touch the tech
  • start small and measure. if the first one saves five hours a week the second is easy to say yes to
plain-english summary
no-shows and review admin are usually the fastest wins. both pay back inside a month
menus synced across delivery apps stop chargebacks and save the manager an hour a day
supplier reorders and invoices give back around half a day a week each
shift planning from your POS gets sunday nights back and makes new managers safer to leave on their own
